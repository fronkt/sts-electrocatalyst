## Why Ni31 and Cu22 cast badly: working explanations (not confirmed)

Both look like the mold did not fill completely (misruns): thin, necked and stepped sections, laps, a peeled skin and pits. The photos alone cannot settle the cause.

1. **Ni31Cr29Cu5Mn35: the most Cr and Mn together** (29 + 35 at.%).
   - Dissolving Cr (melting point 1,907 °C) needs a hot melt, and a hot melt boils off Mn (boiling point 2,061 °C).
   - A melt held cooler to save Mn reaches the mold with little superheat and freezes before the mold fills.
   - Mn fume and Mn/Cr oxide films also thicken the melt. The frosted grey coating is consistent with condensed Mn fume or oxide.
   - Cu8 has the same Mn but less Cr (23 at.%) and filled its mold, so this is a matter of degree.
2. **Cu22Fe30Co32Mn15: copper that won't mix.** This alloy has no Cr. Its 22 at.% Cu sits with Fe and Co, which barely dissolve Cu; Cu–Fe and Cu–Co even separate into two liquids when slightly undercooled.
   - The Fe–Co-rich metal starts freezing near the Fe–Co liquidus, while a Cu- and Mn-rich liquid stays molten hundreds of degrees lower.
   - The melt is mushy over a wide range and stops flowing. The step looks like the point where it stopped filling at full diameter.
   - Mn adds fume and pits.
3. **Cu26Ni9Cr31Co33 (not flagged, but visible).** Its copper-coloured skin is the same immiscibility seen from outside: Cu-rich liquid separating from Co and Cr and coating the surface.
4. **The crucible.** Its material is not known here.
   - A ceramic crucible (alumina, zirconia) reacts with Mn-rich melts and forms slag.
   - A graphite crucible adds carbon, which forms Cr carbides.
   - Either would thicken the melt and leave inclusions.
   - *Update, 2026-10-17:* the returned scraps suggest a water-cooled copper crucible, which hardly reacts with a melt. If that is confirmed, this explanation becomes unlikely (see "The scraps", item 1).

**What would tell these apart:**
- Song's run notes: melter, crucible material, charge mass, superheat or power, pour method, remelts, and what went to scrap.
- Cross-sections: porosity, laps, undissolved Cr, Cu-rich phase, oxide or carbide inclusions.
- EDS of the frosted coating: Mn-rich means fume.

**What the scraps show (working reading, not confirmed).**
1. **The shells look like crucible skulls.** A skull is metal that froze against the crucible wall and stayed behind when the melt was poured. The smooth bowl shape with a tail (the pour lip), together with the "ARC" IDs, suggests arc melting in a water-cooled copper crucible with a tilt pour. Confirm the melter and crucible with Song. If confirmed:
   - every melt leaves a skull, so much of the "scrap" is normal for the method, not a sign that the melt failed;
   - a water-cooled copper crucible hardly reacts with the melt, so explanation 4 becomes unlikely;
   - the arc's hot spot boils Mn off readily, which supports explanation 1.
2. **The coating follows Mn.**
   - All three Mn alloys have grey or dark coatings: Ni31 and Cu8 (35 at.% Mn) heavily, Cu22 (15 at.%) less.
   - The three Mn-free alloys (Fe25, Ni34, Cu26) are bright metal or copper-coloured.
   - This fits Mn fume and Mn oxide; MnO is green, like the patch on Ni31.
   - Mn evaporation means Mn loss. In Cu8 and Ni31, Mn is therefore the element most likely to miss the ±2 at.% acceptance (processing plan, step 6).
3. **The copper colour follows Cu without Ni.**
   - Cu26 (26 at.% Cu, 9 at.% Ni) is copper-pink.
   - Ni34 (29 at.% Cu, 34 at.% Ni) is silver, although it has more Cu.
   - Cu dissolves completely in Ni but barely in Co, Cr or Fe, so Cu separates where there is little Ni to hold it.
   - Cu22 (no Ni) is reddish-brown at one edge, though its Mn coating hides most of its surface.
   - This fits explanations 2 and 3. It makes Cu26 the alloy most likely to keep a Cu-rich second phase after homogenization; step 6's BSE should look for one.
4. **Correction: the skulls are not a conservative local-melting test.**
   - What was said: earlier today this record and the processing plan said a scrap that cooled slowly is coarser and more segregated than the rods.
   - What the scraps actually are: thin shells, probably chilled against the crucible, so their structure is likely finer than the rods', not coarser. Their composition may also differ from the rods' (Mn lost at the surface, metal frozen first).
   - Consequently:
     - the as-cast reference comes from each alloy's as-cast keeper rod, not from a skull;
     - the skulls serve for EDS of the coatings and deposits (is the coating Mn-rich? is Fe25's black deposit oxide or carbon? what is the white deposit on Cu22?) and as spare material;
     - a local-melting check, if one is ever needed (for example, before annealing a Ni31 re-melt), uses an as-cast rod offcut.
5. **Weigh everything:** every skull, stick, keeper and offcut, and count the saw cuts. Against the charge mass, the total bounds the melt loss (processing plan, step 1).
   - Dry sawing turns roughly 0.5–1 g per cut into swarf on a rod about 10 mm across (kerf 1–1.5 mm), so the bound is loose.
   - It still catches a loss of several grams.

**Quench choice (open, 2026-10-17).** Freeze §5 says "then quench" and does not fix the medium.
- **Why fast cooling matters.** Slow cooling through about 900–500 °C can grow Cu-rich particles (Cu8, Cu22, Cu26, Ni34) and gives Cr- and Mn-rich alloys time to form σ.
- **Cracking risk.** FCC CoCrFeNi-type alloys are routinely water-quenched from 1,000–1,200 °C after homogenization. Cracks would start at casting defects (the laps and necks in Ni31 and Cu22), which are not disk material.
- **Options:** water (the plan), warm water (about 60–80 °C) or oil, which are milder and still a quench; cooling in air or Ar (a weak quench, better recorded as a departure); furnace cooling (a departure).
- All six alloys are in one boat, so they cool the same way whichever is chosen.

**Notes (2026-10-17).**
- **Oxidation during the transfer.** The seconds between furnace and water grow a thin oxide film (microns), far less than the machining allowance (1.5–3 mm on the thick rods, at least 0.5 mm on faces, about 0.25 mm on Ni31's thin sections).
- **No further heat treatment.** The homogenization and quench are the only heat treatment in freeze §5. The one exception is a Ni31 re-melt, which would get its own 1,050 °C / 24 h anneal.
- **No local-melting check before this anneal.** None is recorded (processing plan, step 3). Step 6's BSE therefore checks for local melting instead, especially in Cu22, Cu26 and Cu8: rounded interdendritic pores, re-solidified pockets, and Cu-rich films along grain boundaries.
- **XRD may not be possible at FWM; EDS will be** (Frank, 2026-10-17). Acceptance (EDS, ±2 at.%) is unaffected. One homogenized slice per alloy is kept for XRD elsewhere before the report lock. EBSD on FWM's SEM, if available, gives the phases in the meantime (processing plan, step 6).
